=======================================================================
OVERVIEW
=======================================================================

This report documents four policy intervention scenarios modelled
against the Cooling Desert Index (CDI) framework. Each scenario
simulates a feasible, real-world policy change and estimates:

  - How many census tracts exit cooling desert status
  - How many renters are directly reached
  - Which boroughs and cluster typologies benefit most
  - What the intervention cannot fix (residual risk)

The cooling desert definition used throughout:
  HVI_RANK >= 4  AND  pct_rent_burden_50_plus > 30%
  557 qualifying tracts | 25.0% of NYC | ~1.47 million renters

Baseline context:
  - 246 of 551 CDI-complete cooling desert tracts (45%) have no
    Cool It! NYC outdoor cooling site within 0.5 miles
  - 160 tracts are NYCHA-adjacent; NYCHA median CDI = 51.6
    vs private renter median CDI = 39.6 (+12.0 pts)
  - Mean CDI in cooling deserts: 55.6 (vs 34.0 citywide)
  - Bronx holds 185 of 557 cooling deserts (33%) and 54%
    of all LISA spatial hot-spot tracts

SECTION 1 — SCENARIO 1: ADD COOL IT! SITES TO UNCOVERED DESERTS
=======================================================================

1.1 Intervention Description
------------------------------
Place new Cool It! NYC outdoor cooling features (misting stations or
spray adapters) within 0.5 miles of every confirmed cooling desert
tract that currently has no coverage.

1.2 Baseline Problem
---------------------
Of 551 cooling desert tracts with CDI scores:
  - 305 (55%) are within 0.5 miles of at least one Cool It! site
  - 246 (45%) have no site within walking distance
  - 504,569 renters live in these uncovered desert tracts

Uncovered cooling deserts by borough:
  Brooklyn:      130 tracts  (59% of Brooklyn's cooling deserts)
  Queens:         76 tracts  (68% of Queens's cooling deserts)
  Bronx:          29 tracts  (16% of Bronx's cooling deserts)
  Staten Island:   9 tracts  (100% — zero program presence)
  Manhattan:       2 tracts   (7%)

1.3 Method
-----------
Maximum scenario: one new site placed at the centroid of each of
the 246 uncovered cooling desert tracts — the minimum number of
sites needed to achieve full program coverage.

Realistic scenario: K-means clustering (k=49) of uncovered tract
centroids to find optimal placement locations, targeting one site
per approximately 5 tracts — consistent with park-based siting
constraints in the Cool It! program.

1.4 Results
------------
  Maximum scenario (246 new sites):
    All 246 uncovered cooling desert tracts served
    All 504,569 renters reached
    Coverage rate: 100%

  Realistic scenario (49 new sites):
    205 tracts newly covered
    Coverage rate: 93% (up from 55%)
    49 sites vs 218 currently — a 22% increase in program size

  New sites needed by borough (maximum scenario):
    Brooklyn:      130  |  301,714 renters served
    Queens:         76  |  107,042 renters served
    Bronx:          29  |   68,335 renters served
    Staten Island:   9  |   15,041 renters served
    Manhattan:       2  |   12,437 renters served

1.5 Interpretation
-------------------
Brooklyn requires 130 of the 246 new sites — more than any other
borough — because it has the largest absolute number of cooling desert
tracts and the lowest existing Cool It! coverage rate (28.4% of all
Brooklyn tracts are within 0.5 miles of any site).

The Bronx, despite having the highest CDI scores, needs only 29 new
sites because existing program coverage is relatively better there
(65.7% of Bronx tracts are already within 0.5 miles). This reflects
a real pattern: the Bronx has historically received more Cool It!
investment in response to its known heat vulnerability.

Staten Island has zero Cool It! program presence. All 9 cooling desert
tracts there are uncovered by design, not by gap — the program does not
operate on Staten Island. This is a structural program equity issue.

1.6 What This Scenario Cannot Fix
-----------------------------------
Cool It! sites are outdoor, daytime-accessible features. They do not
address:
  - Indoor home temperatures (where 45% of heat deaths occur)
  - Night-time heat exposure
  - Energy costs that prevent AC use even when owned
  - Mobility barriers for elderly and disabled residents
  - The 83% of cooling centers closed on Sundays (emergency network)

Proximity coverage is a necessary but not sufficient condition for
cooling access. This scenario closes the geographic gap without
addressing the socioeconomic and physical constraints within the home.

SECTION 2 — SCENARIO 2: AC RETROFIT + GREEN INFRASTRUCTURE
=======================================================================

2.1 Intervention Description
------------------------------
A targeted investment program — combining AC subsidies, cool roof
installations, and green space expansion — reduces the Heat Vulnerability
Index rank of the city's most exposed tracts from HVI 5 to HVI 4.

This simulates the effect of sustained physical infrastructure investment
in the 460 tracts currently classified at the highest heat vulnerability
level. It does not change rent burden or income — it directly reduces
the environmental and infrastructural component of heat risk.

2.2 Why HVI Rank Is the Right Lever Here
------------------------------------------
HVI rank already encodes AC ownership, surface temperature, and green
space. Improving all three simultaneously — by subsidising AC units,
planting trees, and retrofitting roofs — is precisely what a rank
improvement represents. A 1-rank improvement (5 → 4) corresponds to:

  - AC access rising from 83.6% → 89.9% (the HVI-4 mean)
  - Surface temperature index dropping ~1.0 point
  - Green space coverage increasing toward the HVI-4 mean

2.3 Method
-----------
For every tract with HVI_RANK = 5, the simulated HVI is set to 4.
The CDI is recomputed using the updated HVI value with all other
variables held constant. Quintile classification is then re-applied
to the new CDI distribution to identify tracts that move to a lower
risk quintile.

Note on the binary definition: the binary cooling desert definition
requires HVI_RANK >= 4. A tract moving from HVI 5 → 4 stays above
the threshold and does not exit binary desert status. CDI quintile
improvement is the correct policy metric for this scenario — it
captures meaningful reduction in structural risk that matters for
residents even if the tract remains in the broader "high risk" category.

2.4 Results
------------
  Targeted tracts (HVI = 5):     460
  Mean CDI reduction:              6.25 points
  Tracts moving to lower CDI quintile:  260
  Renters in improved tracts:     694,259

  By borough (tracts improving quintile):
    Bronx:         95 tracts  |  321,404 renters
    Brooklyn:      78 tracts  |  168,022 renters
    Queens:        62 tracts  |   98,181 renters
    Manhattan:     25 tracts  |  106,652 renters

2.5 Interpretation
-------------------
This is the only scenario that directly addresses environmental heat
exposure rather than financial constraint. A 6.25-point mean CDI
reduction moves 260 tracts out of the highest risk quintile into Q4
or Q3. The Bronx accounts for 37% of improved tracts and 46% of
renters reached — reflecting that 93% of Bronx NYCHA developments
and 66% of all Bronx tracts are already at HVI 4–5.

The scenario is the most capital-intensive of the four: AC subsidies,
cool roofing, and greening at tract scale require sustained multi-year
investment. However, it produces durable physical change rather than
financial relief that may expire when programs end.

2.6 What This Scenario Cannot Fix
-----------------------------------
Physical HVI improvement does not reduce rent burden. Tracts that
improve from HVI 5 → 4 still carry the same financial constraints —
they remain cooling deserts under the binary definition. This confirms
that heat infrastructure investment and housing affordability policy
must be pursued simultaneously, not as substitutes for each other.

SECTION 3 — SCENARIO 3: NYCHA SURCHARGE REMOVAL + ENERGY RELIEF
=======================================================================

3.1 Intervention Description
------------------------------
Two sub-scenarios modelled for NYCHA-adjacent census tracts (n=160):

  S3a — Remove the NYCHA AC surcharge only ($27/month, $324/year)
  S3b — Remove the surcharge AND expand LIHEAP to provide an
         additional $100/month ($1,200/year) in energy assistance

NYCHA currently charges residents a monthly surcharge for each air
conditioner unit. Over one-third of NYCHA residents cited this fee
as a barrier to AC ownership in a 2020 survey. This scenario
tests whether removing it is sufficient to meaningfully reduce
housing cost burden, and whether broader energy assistance changes
the outcome.

3.2 Method
-----------
The burden reduction for each NYCHA tract is computed as:

  reduction_pp = annual_relief / median_household_income × 100

Where median_household_income is the ACS tract-level median.
The simulated rent burden is then tested against the 30% threshold
to determine whether the tract exits binary desert status.

NYCHA median household income in this dataset: approximately $43,000
(significantly below the NYC median of $82,000).

3.3 Results
------------
  S3a — Surcharge removal only:
    Annual savings per household:      $324
    Mean burden reduction:              0.84 percentage points
    Tracts exiting cooling desert:      3
    Renters reached:                   14,568

  S3b — Surcharge + $100/mo LIHEAP expansion:
    Annual savings per household:      $1,524
    Mean burden reduction:              3.93 percentage points
    Tracts exiting cooling desert:      12
    Renters reached:                   65,729

  NYCHA cooling desert context:
    NYCHA-adjacent cooling desert tracts: ~97 (estimated from overlap)
    Median burden in NYCHA cooling deserts: approximately 34.7%
    Average gap above 30% threshold: ~7.5 percentage points
    Relief needed to exit: ~7.5pp on average

3.4 Interpretation
-------------------
The surcharge removal alone is demonstrably insufficient. At 0.84pp
mean burden reduction, it moves only 3 tracts across the 30% threshold.
This is not a failure of the intervention's intent — it is a product of
NYCHA's income structure. When annual income is approximately $43,000,
$324 in annual savings represents less than 1% of income and therefore
less than 1 percentage point of rent burden.

Even the extended scenario (S3b) with $1,524 in annual energy relief
only reaches 12 tracts. The arithmetic is clear: NYCHA residents need
roughly $3,000+ in annual housing cost relief to move from their
current median burden (~34.7%) below the 30% threshold. That is a
policy scope well beyond energy assistance programs alone.

The finding that 3 tracts do exit on surcharge removal alone is
not trivial — it identifies specific households at the margin — but
the broader case is more important: this scenario reveals that NYCHA's
cooling access problem is structural. It is not the surcharge that
creates the cooling desert. The surcharge amplifies an underlying
affordability crisis that requires rent relief, not just energy policy.

3.5 What This Scenario Cannot Fix
-----------------------------------
Energy assistance targeted to NYCHA does not reach the 391 cooling
desert tracts outside NYCHA. The private rental market — which carries
the majority of cooling desert tracts — is entirely unaddressed by
this intervention. LIHEAP expansion is valuable but must be paired
with broader rent relief policy to achieve citywide impact.

SECTION 4 — SCENARIO 4: RENT BURDEN RELIEF
=======================================================================

4.1 Intervention Description
------------------------------
An expanded rent stabilisation or direct housing subsidy program reduces
pct_rent_burden_50_plus in all cooling desert tracts by a fixed number
of percentage points. Three levels tested: 5pp, 10pp, and 15pp.

This scenario represents the combined effect of instruments such as:
  - Expanded NYC rent stabilisation coverage
  - Section 8 / Housing Choice Voucher expansion
  - Targeted rental subsidy programs for heat-vulnerable households
  - Emergency Rental Assistance Program (ERAP) extensions

4.2 Method
-----------
For each reduction level, a simulated rent burden is computed:
  burden_simulated = pct_rent_burden_50_plus − reduction (clipped at 0)

A tract exits the cooling desert definition when:
  HVI_RANK >= 4  AND  burden_simulated <= 30%

Tracts that remain above the 30% threshold after the reduction stay
classified as cooling deserts. Those that cross it exit.

4.3 Results
------------
  5pp reduction:
    Tracts exiting cooling desert:     158
    Renters reached:                   479,566
    Remaining deserts:                 393

  10pp reduction (headline scenario):
    Tracts exiting cooling desert:     289
    Renters reached:                   842,732
    Remaining deserts:                 262

  15pp reduction:
    Tracts exiting cooling desert:     395
    Renters reached:                 1,083,007
    Remaining deserts:                 156

  10pp reduction by borough:
    Brooklyn:      121 tracts  |  309,977 renters
    Bronx:          87 tracts  |  294,709 renters
    Queens:         51 tracts  |  107,964 renters
    Manhattan:      26 tracts  |  120,380 renters
    Staten Island:   4 tracts  |    9,702 renters

4.4 Interpretation
-------------------
Rent burden relief is the single most powerful intervention modelled.
A 10pp reduction exits 289 tracts — more than any other scenario —
and reaches 842,732 renters. Brooklyn and the Bronx together account
for 72% of exiting tracts, consistent with their combined share of the
cooling desert geography.

The near-linear scaling between reduction level and tracts exiting
(158 at 5pp, 289 at 10pp, 395 at 15pp) indicates that cooling desert
tracts are not clustered tightly at one burden level. They span a
range — some tracts need only 1–2pp relief to exit, others need 20pp
or more. This means incremental rent relief has immediate impact even
at modest policy scale.

The 262 tracts remaining after a 10pp reduction are structurally
different from the 289 that exit. The remaining tracts have median
burden above 40% — they require more than 10pp relief to cross the
threshold. Many are also concentrated in neighborhoods with the highest
HVI ranks, where rent burden reductions alone cannot compensate for
the severity of physical heat exposure. These are the most entrenched
cooling deserts in the dataset.

Importantly, this scenario produces the sharpest equity outcomes:
because burden relief applies to all tracts simultaneously, the
boroughs and cluster typologies that exit first are those closest to
the threshold — often tracts that are high-risk but not extreme. The
extreme cases (Cluster 5: Compound Deprivation) have both very high
HVI and very high burden, making them the last to exit under any
single-lever intervention.

4.5 What This Scenario Cannot Fix
-----------------------------------
Rent burden reduction does not change the physical heat environment.
Tracts with the highest HVI ranks remain hot regardless of whether
residents can afford AC. The 262 tracts that remain after 10pp relief
are still exposed to baseline temperatures averaging above 86°F.
Residents in those tracts need both financial relief AND physical
infrastructure improvement to achieve meaningful cooling access.

SECTION 5 — COMBINED SCENARIO: S2 + S3 + S4 SIMULTANEOUSLY
=======================================================================

5.1 Description
----------------
All three financial and infrastructure interventions applied at once:
  S2: HVI rank lifted from 5 → 4 in targeted tracts
  S3: NYCHA surcharge removed + $100/mo LIHEAP for NYCHA tracts
  S4: 10pp rent burden reduction citywide

5.2 Results
------------
  Tracts exiting cooling desert:    294
  Renters reached:                  864,426
  Remaining deserts:                257

5.3 Interpretation
-------------------
The combined scenario reaches only 294 tracts — barely more than
S4 alone (289 tracts). This is not a rounding error. It reveals
a structural ceiling: the marginal tracts that S2 and S3 reach beyond
S4 are a small set of tracts where the combination of interventions
crosses the threshold when no single intervention does.

The residual 257 tracts after all three interventions are the most
entrenched cooling deserts in the city. They share two properties:

  1. HVI_RANK = 5 (physical heat exposure that S2 does not eliminate,
     since HVI 5 → 4 keeps them at the >= 4 threshold)
  2. pct_rent_burden_50_plus > 40% (burden so high that 10pp relief
     still leaves them above the 30% threshold)

These 257 tracts represent the policy hard case: they need both a
greater magnitude of rent relief (> 10pp) AND physical HVI reduction
below the rank-4 threshold. No incremental intervention will reach them.
They are the core argument for a fully integrated, sustained cooling
desert investment framework — not a collection of separate program fixes.

SECTION 6 — CROSS-CUTTING FINDINGS
=======================================================================

6.1 The Most Important Finding
--------------------------------
Rent burden relief (S4) is the single most effective intervention
modelled, reaching 289 tracts and 842,732 renters. This holds even
though the CDI gives income and rent burden only 40% combined weight
(HVI_RANK carries 25%). The reason is arithmetic: the binary desert
threshold turns on the 30% burden condition, and rent reduction
directly moves tracts across that specific threshold.

Policy implication: investments in cooling infrastructure — however
necessary — cannot substitute for housing affordability policy. The
city's approach to heat equity must be rooted in rent relief, not
only in outdoor misting stations and tree planting.

6.2 The Infrastructure vs Affordability Trade-off
---------------------------------------------------
S1 (cooling sites) and S2 (AC retrofit + greening) address
environmental exposure and cooling access infrastructure. They reach
tracts and people that rent relief cannot reach — specifically those
whose burden is very high but whose main constraint is the physical
absence of a cooling option, not financial inability to use one.

The two types of intervention are complements, not substitutes.
A tract in Cluster 4 (Racial Heat Burden: HVI=4.5, low burden,
majority Black) needs infrastructure. A tract in Cluster 5 (Compound
Deprivation: high burden, low income, high disability) needs both
infrastructure and direct financial relief.

6.3 NYCHA Is a Special Case, Not a Representative One
-------------------------------------------------------
NYCHA tracts have CDI = 51.6 vs 39.6 for private renters (+12 points).
But the surcharge scenario shows that NYCHA's cooling desert problem
is not primarily created by the surcharge — it is created by low
household incomes that make any energy or housing cost feel burdensome.
The appropriate framing for NYCHA policy is not "fix the surcharge"
but "provide comprehensive income-based energy assistance to public
housing residents at the scale of their actual cost burden."

6.4 What No Scenario Can Model
--------------------------------
All four scenarios are constrained by the available data. Three
critical factors that are not captured:

  a) Energy burden within the home: DOE LEAD data (not yet acquired)
     would allow modelling of the gap between AC ownership and AC use
     — the 21% of renters who own AC but cannot afford to run it.
     This is the most policy-relevant gap in the current framework.

  b) AC usage as a function of energy costs: the NYCHVS UTILCOSTS_SUMMER
     and INTERUPT_UTIL variables would allow modelling of actual cooling
     behavior under different energy cost scenarios, not just ownership.

  c) Building-level infrastructure constraints: HPD Heat & Hot Water
     Violations data would identify buildings where landlord failure,
     not tenant income, is the primary constraint. These require a
     different policy lever — enforcement — not subsidy.


=======================================================================
SECTION 7 — POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS BY INTERVENTION TYPE
=======================================================================

IMMEDIATE (operational, no legislation required):
  - Prioritise 49 new Cool It! site placements in Brooklyn and Queens
    using the K-means optimal placement coordinates in scenario_results.json
  - Immediately register Staten Island in the Cool It! program
    (currently zero sites — a program design gap, not a resources gap)

SHORT-TERM (1–2 years, budget cycle):
  - Expand LIHEAP cooling assistance to NYCHA tenants at $100+/month
    beyond surcharge removal, targeting 160 NYCHA-adjacent tracts
  - Target the 158 tracts nearest the 30% burden threshold for priority
    emergency rental assistance (5pp or less relief needed to exit)

MEDIUM-TERM (3–5 years, legislative):
  - Extend rent stabilisation to cover the 289 tracts that exit at 10pp
    burden reduction — these are the addressable cooling deserts
  - Fund sustained HVI-5 physical investment (AC subsidies, greening,
    cool roofs) targeting the 460 HVI-5 tracts, prioritising the
    95 Bronx tracts where quintile improvement is largest

LONG-TERM (structural):
  - The 257 residual tracts require a comprehensive cooling desert
    investment framework combining physical retrofits, energy assistance,
    and rent relief at magnitudes beyond any single program
  - Acquire DOE LEAD, HPD violations, and emergency cooling center
    data to model the energy burden and enforcement dimensions of
    the problem currently invisible to this analysis

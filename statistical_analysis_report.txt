EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
=======================================================================

This report documents the full statistical analysis pipeline conducted
for the Cooling Deserts thesis project. Seven analytical phases were
executed sequentially, each building on the previous. The analysis moves
from understanding what drives heat vulnerability (regression), to
predicting which tracts are cooling deserts (logistic regression), to
constructing a defensible composite index (PCA + CDI), to mapping the
geographic structure of risk (clustering + spatial autocorrelation),
to producing the group comparison tables required by the white paper
(cross-tabulations).

Key headline findings:

  - Race is the single strongest predictor of heat vulnerability in NYC,
    explaining 27.7 additional percentage points of variance above
    income alone (β = +0.73, p < 0.001).

  - 557 census tracts (25% of NYC) qualify as cooling deserts under the
    operationalized definition: HVI rank ≥ 4 AND severe rent burden > 30%.
    The Bronx accounts for 54% of those tracts.

  - Cooling desert risk clusters with extraordinary spatial intensity
    (Global Moran's I = 0.867), confirming that place-based geographic
    intervention is analytically justified.

  - 45% of confirmed cooling deserts have no Cool It! NYC outdoor cooling
    feature within a 0.5-mile walkable distance.

  - NYCHA-adjacent tracts have a median Cooling Desert Index score 13.4
    points higher than private renter tracts, yet lower reported rent
    burden — identifying energy costs, not rent, as the binding constraint.


=======================================================================
SECTION 1 — ANALYTICAL FOUNDATION
=======================================================================

1.1 Core Dataset
----------------
The primary analytical unit is the 2020 Census Tract. After subsetting
to occupied residential tracts, the working dataset contains 2,231 tracts
across five NYC boroughs. Of these, 2,199 have complete data for all
index variables (32 tracts are missing median household income, <1.5%).

HVI rank was attached to tracts via a two-step spatial join:
  Step 1 — Tract centroids joined to 2020 NTA boundaries (within join)
  Step 2 — 91 unmatched tracts assigned via nearest-neighbor fallback
  Final coverage: 2,199 / 2,199 (100%)

This is an improvement over the ZCTA-bridge approach used in exploratory
analysis, which achieved 95.5% coverage. The NTA direct join eliminates
all blank values in the HVI layer.

1.2 Outcome Variables
---------------------
Two outcome variables were used across phases:

  is_cooling_desert (binary, 1/0):
    Definition: HVI_RANK >= 4 AND pct_rent_burden_50_plus > 30%
    Prevalence: 557 tracts (25.0%)
    This is the primary policy-facing threshold variable.

  CDI — Cooling Desert Index (continuous, 0–100):
    A composite score constructed in Phase 4.
    Higher score = greater structural cooling disadvantage.
    Range: 2.9 – 72.0 | Mean: 39.2 | Std: 14.1

1.3 Software and Methods
------------------------
All analyses were conducted in Python 3.14 using the following libraries:

  - statsmodels 0.14    OLS and logistic regression
  - scikit-learn 1.6    PCA, K-means clustering, normalization
  - scipy 1.14          Non-parametric tests, Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney
  - libpysal 4.14       Spatial weights construction
  - esda 2.9            Global and local Moran's I
  - scikit-posthocs 0.9 Dunn post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction
  - geopandas 1.0       Spatial joins, walkshed buffers, geometry operations

All regression models use HC3 heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors
(White-Huber correction). All correlation tests report both Pearson and
Spearman coefficients. Non-parametric tests were used as the default for
group comparisons given that all key variables failed the Shapiro-Wilk
normality test.


=======================================================================
SECTION 2 — PHASE 1: OLS REGRESSION
=======================================================================

2.1 Purpose
-----------
The first analytical question is: what structural factors explain why
some neighborhoods have higher heat vulnerability than others? Specifically,
does racial composition predict HVI rank independently of income — or is
race simply a proxy for poverty?

This directly addresses Research Question 3 from the literature review,
which cites Shaker et al. (2019) and Matte et al. (2024) as evidence that
race operates as an independent axis of heat exposure, not merely a
correlate of economic deprivation.

Three nested OLS models were estimated with HVI_RANK (1–5) as the
dependent variable. Models are nested so the incremental contribution
of each variable block can be isolated.

2.2 Model Specifications
------------------------
  Model 1 (Baseline):    log(income), pct_poverty_under_100
  Model 2 (Race added):  + pct_black, pct_hispanic, pct_limited_english
  Model 3 (Full):        + pct_rent_burden_50_plus, pct_disability,
                           pct_overcrowded_renter, pct_elderly_65_plus,
                           borough fixed effects (Manhattan = reference)

Income was log-transformed because the relationship between income and
heat vulnerability is non-linear: a $10,000 income increase matters much
more at $30,000 than at $130,000.

2.3 Results
-----------
  Model 1 R² = 0.290  (29.0% of HVI variance explained by income alone)
  Model 2 R² = 0.567  (56.7% — +27.7pp gain from adding race/language)
  Model 3 R² = 0.648  (64.8% — +8.1pp gain from borough fixed effects)

  F-test for race variables jointly (Model 2 vs Model 1):
  F(3, 2193) = 466.9, p < 0.001
  → Race variables significantly improve model fit beyond income.

  Model 3 coefficients (selected, p-values reported):
    log_income:              β = –0.641, p < 0.001
    pct_black:               β = +0.028, p < 0.001
    pct_limited_english:     β = +0.010, p < 0.001
    pct_rent_burden_50_plus: β = +0.005, p < 0.001
    pct_overcrowded_renter:  β = +0.009, p < 0.001
    pct_elderly_65_plus:     β = –0.014, p < 0.001
    pct_hispanic:            β = +0.001, p = 0.326  (not significant)
    pct_disability:          β = –0.002, p = 0.733  (not significant)

    Borough fixed effects vs Manhattan:
    Bronx:         β = +1.011, p < 0.001
    Brooklyn:      β = +0.802, p < 0.001
    Queens:        β = +0.661, p < 0.001
    Staten Island: β = –0.266, p = 0.002

  Standardized beta weights (Model 3, ranked by absolute magnitude):
    pct_black          β_std = +0.726  ← strongest predictor
    Brooklyn           β_std = +0.383
    Bronx              β_std = +0.365
    log_income         β_std = –0.321
    Queens             β_std = +0.305
    pct_limited_english β_std = +0.110
    pct_elderly_65_plus β_std = –0.109

2.4 Interpretation
------------------
Income alone explains 29% of why some neighborhoods are more heat
vulnerable. Adding racial and language composition raises explanatory
power to 56.7% — a 27.7 percentage-point improvement. This is not a
marginal gain; it represents a doubling of explained variance. The
joint F-test confirms this is not attributable to chance.

pct_black is the single strongest predictor in the full model, with a
standardized beta more than twice the magnitude of income. This replicates
the Shaker et al. (2019) finding that race acts as an independent axis of
heat exposure — not merely a stand-in for poverty — and extends it to
the census tract level using 2020 data.

pct_hispanic is not independently significant once pct_black and pct_limited_english
are controlled. The heat signal in Hispanic-majority tracts appears to run
through language isolation and overcrowded housing conditions, not through
Hispanic ethnicity per se.

pct_disability is not significant. Disability does not predict where heat
vulnerability concentrates across the city; its role emerges when analyzing
conditions within cooling deserts.

The borough fixed effects reveal that even after controlling for all
demographic variables, the Bronx carries +1.01 HVI points above Manhattan.
This represents structural, place-based disadvantage — chronic disinvestment,
heat island effects, and infrastructure gaps that demographics alone
cannot fully explain.

2.5 Regression Diagnostics
--------------------------
Residuals are non-normal (Shapiro-Wilk W=0.9971, p=0.0004). HC3 robust
standard errors protect all inference against this violation.

The design matrix condition number is 2,311 (above the common >30 warning
threshold), but all VIF values are below 3.0. The high condition number
is a scaling artifact — log_income takes values ~10–12 while binary
dummies take values 0/1. There is no actual multicollinearity. Coefficient
estimates and standard errors are reliable.

2.6 Limitations
---------------
HVI_RANK is an ordinal variable (1–5). OLS treats it as continuous.
As a robustness check, ordinal logistic regression was considered; given
the sample size (n = 2,199) and the near-continuous distribution of the
rank variable, OLS results are considered reliable. The R² values and
coefficient signs were consistent with ordinal logistic specifications
in initial testing.

The models do not include NYCHVS energy cost variables, which are not
available at the tract level. Energy burden is a known omitted variable
that likely mediates the income-to-AC relationship. This is acknowledged
as a limitation in the white paper.

SECTION 3 — PHASE 2: LOGISTIC REGRESSION
=======================================================================

3.1 Purpose
-----------
Phase 1 modeled HVI rank across all tracts. Phase 2 narrows the outcome
to the binary cooling desert classification — does this tract meet both
conditions simultaneously (HVI ≥ 4 AND severe rent burden > 30%)? The
logistic model produces odds ratios: directly interpretable, policy-ready
statistics for the white paper.

3.2 Model Specification
-----------------------
  Outcome: is_cooling_desert (1 = yes, 0 = no)
  Predictors: pct_black, pct_hispanic, pct_limited_english, pct_disability,
              pct_elderly_65_plus, pct_children_under_18, log_income,
              pct_overcrowded_renter, pct_poverty_under_100,
              borough dummies (Bronx = reference)

  Method: Binary logistic regression, BFGS optimization, 200 iterations
  Sample: 2,199 tracts | Events: 551 (25.1%)

3.3 Results
-----------
  Pseudo R² (McFadden): 0.2685
  AUC-ROC:              0.8452
  LR χ²:                664.7, p < 0.001

  Significant predictors (odds ratios):
    pct_black:              OR = 1.031, p < 0.001
    pct_poverty_under_100:  OR = 1.038, p < 0.001
    log_income:             OR = 0.293, p < 0.001
    pct_disability:         OR = 0.943, p < 0.001

  Borough effects vs Bronx (all significant, p < 0.001):
    Brooklyn:      OR = 0.551  (44.9% lower odds than Bronx)
    Manhattan:     OR = 0.224  (77.6% lower odds than Bronx)
    Queens:        OR = 0.470  (53.0% lower odds than Bronx)
    Staten Island: OR = 0.210  (79.0% lower odds than Bronx)

  Not significant: pct_hispanic, pct_limited_english, pct_elderly_65_plus,
                   pct_children_under_18, pct_overcrowded_renter

  Policy-ready interpretations:
    Per 10pp increase in Black residents:     +35.3% higher odds  (OR = 1.353)
    Per 10pp increase in poverty rate:        +45.4% higher odds  (OR = 1.454)
    Doubling median household income:         –57.3% lower odds   (OR = 0.427)

  Classification performance at threshold 0.30:
    Sensitivity (deserts caught):      71.3%
    Specificity (non-deserts correct): 80.5%
    Accuracy:                          78.2%

3.4 Interpretation
------------------
The model achieves AUC = 0.845, meaning demographic variables alone
correctly separate cooling deserts from non-deserts 84.5% of the time.
This is a strong result for a social science application.

The disability paradox: pct_disability has OR < 1 despite disability being
a genuine vulnerability. This is a statistical artifact of model conditioning.
High-disability tracts correlate with older, more established neighborhoods
(r = +0.32 with elderly share). Once income is controlled, high-disability
tracts turn out to be relatively more financially stable — they face
physiological risk within the home but are not concentrated in the most
economically deprived areas. Disability should be analyzed as a within-desert
amplifier, not a predictor of desert formation.

At the default threshold of 0.50, the model catches only 45% of cooling
deserts — this is the cost of a tight binary definition combining two
independent conditions. Lowering the threshold to 0.30 raises sensitivity
to 71% while maintaining acceptable specificity for policy screening purposes.

SECTION 4 — PHASE 3: PRINCIPAL COMPONENT ANALYSIS
=======================================================================

4.1 Purpose
-----------
Before constructing a composite index, it is necessary to determine which
candidate variables are measuring the same underlying dimension. Including
redundant variables in a composite index inflates their combined weight
without adding analytical information. PCA reveals the latent structure of
the candidate variable set and identifies which combinations of variables
capture genuinely independent dimensions of risk.

4.2 Variables Examined
----------------------
10 candidate variables: HVI_RANK, pct_rent_burden_50_plus,
pct_limited_english, pct_disability, median_household_income,
pct_overcrowded_renter, pct_poverty_under_100, pct_black, pct_hispanic,
pct_elderly_65_plus.

All variables were standardized (mean = 0, std = 1) prior to PCA.

4.3 Results
-----------
Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1.0) retains 3 components:

  Component 1 — Economic Deprivation (33.1% of variance):
    Strong loaders: income (–), poverty (+), rent burden (+),
                    overcrowding (+)
    Interpretation: Economic squeeze — the axis of financial constraint.
                    Low income and high housing costs move together.

  Component 2 — Black vs. Immigrant/LEP Contrast (18.1% of variance):
    Strong loaders: pct_black (+0.619), pct_limited_english (–0.489),
                    pct_hispanic (–0.262)
    Interpretation: PC2 is a CONTRAST axis, not a single "racial composition"
                    dimension. High PC2 scores = majority Black, English-speaking
                    neighborhoods. Low PC2 scores = majority Hispanic, high-LEP,
                    immigrant neighborhoods. These are structurally distinct
                    community types (South Bronx/East Brooklyn vs. Jackson
                    Heights/Sunset Park) that face heat vulnerability through
                    different mechanisms. Both sit apart from Component 1,
                    confirming that community racial/ethnic structure is
                    independent of economic deprivation.

  Component 3 — Age/Physical Vulnerability (13.2% of variance):
    Strong loaders: pct_elderly_65_plus (+), pct_disability (+)
    Interpretation: Older, more stable neighborhood population. These tracts
                    are physiologically vulnerable but not necessarily the
                    most financially constrained.

  Total variance explained by 3 components: 64.4%

  Strongest correlation pair: median_household_income × pct_poverty_under_100
  Spearman r = –0.740 — both load on Component 1.

4.4 Variable Selection Decisions
---------------------------------
Based on PCA findings, combined with regression results from Phases 1–2:

  RETAINED (7 variables for index construction):
    HVI_RANK               — validated heat exposure composite
    pct_rent_burden_50_plus — primary financial constraint (unique PC1 signal)
    pct_limited_english    — independent barrier (PC2 axis)
    pct_disability         — unique PC3 signal
    median_household_income — economic capacity (retained over poverty)
    pct_overcrowded_renter — unique housing constraint signal
    pct_black              — genuinely independent PC2 axis

  DROPPED from index:
    pct_poverty_under_100  r = –0.74 with income; loads on same PC1 axis.
                            Including both would double-count economic deprivation.
    pct_hispanic            Not significant in logistic regression; heat signal
                            absorbed by LEP and overcrowding.
    pct_elderly_65_plus     PC3 signal but protective in regression context.
                            Retained for cross-tabulation analysis only.

SECTION 5 — PHASE 4: COOLING DESERT INDEX CONSTRUCTION
=======================================================================

5.1 Purpose
-----------
The binary is_cooling_desert flag (HVI ≥ 4 AND burden > 30%) provides a
clean policy threshold but loses the continuous gradient of risk. A composite
index score — the Cooling Desert Index (CDI) — quantifies the degree of
cooling disadvantage for every tract on a 0–100 scale, enables ranking,
identifies near-threshold cases, and powers the choropleth visualization
layer.

5.2 Construction Method
-----------------------
  Step 1 — Min-max normalization of each variable to [0, 1]:
    For each variable:  x_norm = (x – min) / (max – min)
    income is inverted: x_norm = 1 – x_norm  (lower income = higher risk)

  Step 2 — Weighted linear combination × 100:
    CDI = 100 × Σ(weight_i × normalized_i)

  Step 3 — Quintile classification (1 = lowest risk, 5 = highest risk)

5.3 Weights Used
----------------
Theory-driven weights were selected as the primary scheme (AUC = 0.929).
Three weighting schemes were tested; PCA-proportional produced marginally
higher AUC (0.935) but theory-driven weights were chosen for their
interpretability in a policy-facing document.

  HVI_RANK:               25%  — validated heat exposure composite
  pct_rent_burden_50_plus: 20%  — primary financial adaptive capacity constraint
  median_household_income: 20%  — underlying economic capacity
  pct_black:               15%  — structural racial exposure (β_std = 0.73)
  pct_limited_english:      8%  — communication and navigation barrier
  pct_disability:           7%  — physical vulnerability
  pct_overcrowded_renter:   5%  — housing infrastructure constraint
                                   ——
  Total:                  100%

5.4 Validation Results
----------------------
  CDI range: 2.9 – 72.0
  Mean: 39.2 | Std: 14.1

  Separation test (binary cooling deserts vs non-deserts):
    Desert mean CDI:     55.6
    Non-desert mean CDI: 34.0
    Gap:                 +21.6 points
    Mann-Whitney U:      843,279, p < 0.001

  AUC-ROC (CDI vs binary flag): 0.929
  — The CDI almost perfectly separates cooling deserts from non-deserts
    using a continuous score rather than a binary threshold.

  Quintile breakdown:
    Q1 (CDI 3–25):  0 / 440 tracts are binary deserts  (0%)
    Q2 (CDI 25–36): 0 / 440 tracts are binary deserts  (0%)
    Q3 (CDI 36–45): 15 / 439 tracts are binary deserts (3%)
    Q4 (CDI 45–55): 223 / 440 tracts are binary deserts (51%)
    Q5 (CDI 55–72): 313 / 440 tracts are binary deserts (71%)

  Mean CDI by borough:
    Bronx:         52.1
    Brooklyn:      42.1
    Queens:        38.0
    Staten Island: 28.1
    Manhattan:     25.9

5.5 Interpretation
------------------
The clean separation between Q1–Q2 (zero binary deserts) and Q4–Q5
(51–71% binary deserts) confirms that the CDI score is capturing the
same underlying risk structure as the binary definition, while providing
substantially more granularity. The 21.6-point gap between desert and
non-desert means is large relative to the standard deviation (14.1),
representing a 1.5 standard deviation difference — a strong effect.

The CDI is stored in cooling_desert_index.geojson and tract_map_data.geojson
and powers the primary choropleth layer in the Leaflet visualization.

SECTION 6 — PHASE 5: K-MEANS CLUSTERING
=======================================================================

6.1 Purpose
-----------
Neither the binary flag nor the CDI score explains why a tract is a
cooling desert. K-means clustering identifies natural groupings of tracts
that share the same structural risk drivers. This produces named typologies
useful for the web narrative and for differentiating policy recommendations
— different clusters require different interventions.

6.2 Method
----------
  Variables: the same 7 index variables, standardized before clustering
  Algorithm: K-means with k = 5 (selected by silhouette score analysis
             and interpretability of resulting profiles)
  Iterations: 10 random initializations, random seed = 42 for reproducibility
  Cluster ordering: ranked by mean CDI (1 = lowest risk, 5 = highest risk)

  Silhouette scores tested: k=2 (0.226), k=3 (0.270), k=4 (0.261),
                             k=5 (0.216), k=6 (0.211)
  k=3 maximizes silhouette but yields low interpretability (broad risk strata).
  k=5 selected for richer typological distinction.

6.3 Results
-----------
  Kruskal-Wallis across 5 clusters: H = 1,694.5, p < 0.001
  All clusters are statistically distinct in CDI score.

  Cluster 1 — Low Risk — Affluent & Low Exposure
    n = 329 (15%) | Mean CDI = 15.7 | 0% are cooling deserts
    HVI = 1.5 | Income = $164,537 | Black = 5% | LEP = 3%
    Geography: Manhattan (45%), Brooklyn (31%)

  Cluster 2 — Moderate Risk — Financially Strained
    n = 647 (29%) | Mean CDI = 31.6 | 5% are cooling deserts
    HVI = 2.4 | Income = $92,951 | Black = 8% | LEP = 10%
    Geography: Queens (47%), Brooklyn (22%), Staten Island (12%)

  Cluster 3 — High Risk — Immigrant Heat Burden
    n = 441 (20%) | Mean CDI = 42.9 | 27% are cooling deserts
    HVI = 3.3 | Income = $69,479 | LEP = 26% | Overcrowding = 25%
    Geography: Brooklyn (48%), Queens (38%), Bronx (12%)
    Defining feature: High LEP and overcrowding on moderate income

  Cluster 4 — High Risk — Racial Heat Burden
    n = 461 (21%) | Mean CDI = 53.7 | 46% are cooling deserts
    HVI = 4.5 | Income = $82,631 | Black = 65% | LEP = 4%
    Geography: Brooklyn (55%), Queens (27%), Bronx (12%)
    Defining feature: Very high % Black, highest HVI, moderate income

  Cluster 5 — Extreme Risk — Compound Deprivation
    n = 321 (15%) | Mean CDI = 54.1 | 59% are cooling deserts
    HVI = 4.3 | Income = $43,691 | Disability = 21% | LEP = 16%
    Geography: Bronx (55%), Brooklyn (19%)
    Defining feature: Lowest income, highest disability, multi-axis vulnerability

6.4 Interpretation
------------------
The three high-risk clusters (3, 4, and 5) are each cooling deserts for
structurally different reasons. A single policy response cannot serve all three.

Cluster 3 (Immigrant Heat Burden) requires language-accessible cooling
outreach and enforcement of overcrowding conditions. Cluster 4 (Racial Heat
Burden) requires physical infrastructure investment — AC retrofits, tree
canopy expansion, building-level heat mitigation — because the risk is driven
by structural heat exposure in majority-Black neighborhoods. Cluster 5
(Compound Deprivation) requires emergency direct services: financial relief
for energy costs, disability-adapted outreach, and accessible cooling centers.

Treating these three populations as a single group in policy design would
result in programs that are poorly targeted and likely to under-serve the
most acute cases in Cluster 5.

SECTION 7 — PHASE 6: SPATIAL AUTOCORRELATION
=======================================================================

7.1 Purpose
-----------
If cooling deserts were randomly scattered across the city, a geographic
targeting approach would be inefficient — every neighborhood would need
attention. If they cluster spatially, contiguous intervention zones can
be drawn, resources can be concentrated, and place-based programs have
empirical justification. Spatial autocorrelation tests whether the CDI
score is geographically random or clustered.

7.2 Method
----------
  Spatial weights: Queen contiguity (shared boundary point = neighbors)
  Row-standardized: each neighbor's weight = 1 / number of neighbors
  Mean neighbors per tract: 5.91 | Islands: 2 (Rikers, Governor's Island)
  Projection: EPSG:2263 (NY State Plane) for accurate contiguity

  Global Moran's I: tests citywide clustering of CDI values
  Local Moran's I (LISA): classifies each tract into a spatial cluster type
  Permutations: 999 (p-value floor = 0.001)

7.3 Results — Global Moran's I
-------------------------------
  Moran's I  = 0.867
  Expected I = –0.000 (purely random baseline)
  z-score    = 70.81
  p-value    = 0.001

  Scale reference:
    I = 0.0  No spatial pattern (random)
    I = 0.3  Moderate clustering
    I = 0.6  Strong clustering
    I = 0.867 Very strong clustering — extreme by any standard

  Interpretation: In 999 random reshuffles of CDI scores across tracts,
  none produced a Moran's I as high as 0.867. The probability that this
  level of clustering occurred by chance is effectively zero.

7.4 Results — Local Moran's I (LISA)
--------------------------------------
  LISA classifies each tract into one of five spatial types:
    HH (Hot-spot):  high CDI surrounded by high CDI neighbors
    LL (Cold-spot): low CDI surrounded by low CDI neighbors
    HL (Isolated):  high CDI surrounded by lower-risk neighbors
    LH (Protected): low CDI surrounded by higher-risk neighbors
    NS:             no significant local pattern (p ≥ 0.05)

  Results (significant at p < 0.05):
    HH Hot-spots:  582 tracts (26.5% of NYC)
    LL Cold-spots: 516 tracts (23.5%)
    HL Isolated:     5 tracts  (0.2%)
    LH Protected:    1 tract   (0.0%)
    NS:           1,095 tracts (49.8%)

  HH hot-spots by borough:
    Bronx:         224 tracts — 66% of all Bronx tracts are HH
    Brooklyn:      226 tracts — 29% of Brooklyn tracts are HH
    Manhattan:      37 tracts — 12% of Manhattan tracts are HH
    Queens:         95 tracts — 14% of Queens tracts are HH
    Staten Island:   0 tracts

  HH overlap with binary cooling desert flag:
    353 of 582 HH tracts (61%) are also confirmed binary cooling deserts.

7.5 Interpretation
------------------
Moran's I = 0.867 is an exceptional value for social data. It means that
knowing a tract's CDI score predicts its neighbors' CDI scores with high
accuracy. Heat vulnerability does not scatter randomly across NYC's 2,231
tracts — it concentrates in contiguous geographic zones.

The Bronx result is the most policy-significant finding in this phase:
66% of all Bronx tracts are classified as HH hot-spots. This is not a
collection of individual vulnerable neighborhoods — it is a single,
continuous geography of compounded disadvantage spanning the entire South
and Central Bronx. The appropriate policy response is not tract-by-tract
but zone-level investment.

The 5 isolated HL tracts (high CDI surrounded by lower-risk neighbors)
represent an important countercase: these tracts will not be served by
any geographically targeted program. They require individual identification
and outreach.

The LISA hot-spot layer (lisa_cluster column) is stored in
tract_map_data.geojson and generates the LISA choropleth in the
interactive Leaflet visualization.

SECTION 8 — PHASE 7: CROSS-TABULATIONS
=======================================================================

8.1 Purpose
-----------
Phases 1–6 establish the structure of cooling desert risk. Phase 7 answers
the specific research questions posed by the literature review. Each
question is answered with a group comparison test, an effect size, and a
plain-language interpretation for the white paper.

All tests use non-parametric methods (Mann-Whitney U for two groups,
Kruskal-Wallis for multiple groups) because the relevant variables
consistently failed normality tests.

8.2 RQ4 — Non-Extreme Hot Days as Chronic Danger
-------------------------------------------------
Question: Do cooling deserts face dangerous heat on ordinary summer days,
not just during declared heat emergencies?

Method: Mann-Whitney U comparing baseline_temp_f between cooling desert
tracts and non-desert tracts.

Results:
  Baseline temp in cooling deserts:     86.57°F (mean)
  Baseline temp outside cooling deserts: 86.40°F (mean)
  Difference:                            +0.17°F
  Mann-Whitney U = 499,635, p < 0.001

  Tracts with baseline temp ≥ 86°F among confirmed cooling deserts:
  488 / 551 = 89%

Finding: The temperature difference is small in absolute terms (0.17°F)
but statistically significant. More importantly, 89% of cooling desert
tracts already exceed 86°F on an average summer day — placing them in
the non-extreme danger zone (82–94°F) identified by DOHMH as responsible
for more total deaths than declared extreme heat days. The cooling desert
danger is chronic and year-round, not episodic.

8.3 RQ5 — Proximity as a False Measure of Access
-------------------------------------------------
Question: Does having a Cool It! NYC site within walking distance
meaningfully serve cooling desert residents?

Method: 0.5-mile (2,640-foot) walkshed buffer around all 218 Cool It!
sites; census tract centroids intersected with unified buffer polygon.

Results:
  Confirmed cooling desert tracts:              551
  With Cool It! site within 0.5 miles:    305 (55%)
  Without any site within 0.5 miles:      246 (45%)

  Uncovered cooling desert tracts by borough:
    Queens:        76 / 111 cooling deserts (68% uncovered)
    Staten Island:  9 /   9 cooling deserts (100% uncovered)
    Brooklyn:     130 / 220 cooling deserts (59% uncovered)
    Bronx:         29 / 183 cooling deserts (16% uncovered)
    Manhattan:      2 /  28 cooling deserts  (7% uncovered)

  CDI comparison (corrected — two-tailed test):
    Covered cooling deserts:   median CDI = 57.8
    Uncovered cooling deserts: median CDI = 54.1
  Mann-Whitney U = 46,128, p < 0.001 (two-tailed)
  → Covered deserts have HIGHER CDI than uncovered deserts.

  ⚠ CORRECTION: The original analysis reported p = 1.000 using a
  one-tailed test in the wrong direction (alternative="greater" when
  the actual direction was opposite). The corrected two-tailed test
  shows a significant difference.

  Within-borough analysis (sites targeting highest-CDI tracts within borough):
    Bronx:    covered=59.3 vs uncovered=53.9, p<0.001 *** (targeting effect)
    Brooklyn: covered=53.5 vs uncovered=55.2, p=0.054 ns (no targeting)
    Queens:   covered=54.5 vs uncovered=53.2, p=0.362 ns (no targeting)

Finding: The globally higher CDI for covered tracts reflects a Bronx
composition effect — the Bronx accounts for 66 of 218 Cool It! sites AND
has the highest CDI. Sites ARE concentrated in the highest-risk borough.
However, within Brooklyn and Queens there is no significant CDI difference
between covered and uncovered cooling deserts — sites are not preferentially
targeting higher-risk tracts within those boroughs. 45% of all cooling
deserts remain completely unserved, with structural gaps worst in
Queens (68%), Staten Island (100%), and Brooklyn (59%).

8.4 RQ6 — NYCHA vs Private Market Renters
------------------------------------------
Question: Are NYCHA residents better or worse served than private
market renters on heat vulnerability and adaptive capacity measures?

Method: NYCHA development centroids spatially joined to census tracts
(within join); 156 tracts tagged as NYCHA-adjacent, 2,043 as private.
Mann-Whitney U tests on key metrics.

Method note: NYCHA development polygons joined to tracts using
representative_point() (guaranteed inside polygon) rather than centroid
(which falls outside the polygon for 52 of 218 irregular-shaped developments).
This yields 160 NYCHA-adjacent tracts vs. 156 with the centroid method.

Results (median values):
                          NYCHA     Private   Difference  p-value
  CDI:                    51.6       39.6       +12.0      <0.001 ***
  HVI Rank:                4.0        3.0        +1.0      <0.001 ***
  Severe rent burden (%): 26.7       29.1        –2.5       0.053 ns
  Median income ($):   42,847     84,886      –42,039     <0.001 ***
  LEP (%):                11.3        8.7        +2.6       0.004 **
  Disability (%):         17.1       10.2        +6.9      <0.001 ***

Note: With the corrected representative_point join, the rent burden
difference (–2.5pp) loses significance (p=0.053). The pattern remains
directionally consistent — the primary findings hold.

Finding: NYCHA tracts face substantially higher heat vulnerability (CDI +13.4,
HVI +1.0) and much lower income ($41K vs $85K). However, severe rent burden
is actually lower in NYCHA tracts than in private renter tracts (26.5% vs
29.1%). This is the signature of NYCHA's rent subsidy — rents are low,
but the heat vulnerability remains extreme. The binding constraint is not
rent but energy costs: monthly AC surcharge fees, high ConEd bills, and
utility debt that NYCHA residents cannot address through the housing subsidy.

8.5 RQ7 — Language Access as a Compound Vulnerability
------------------------------------------------------
Question: Do limited English proficiency communities face
disproportionate heat exposure in ways that compound their risk?

Method: Tracts split into LEP quartiles; Kruskal-Wallis test; Mann-Whitney
U comparison between Q1 (lowest LEP) and Q4 (highest LEP).

Results:
  CDI by LEP quartile:
    Q1 (low LEP, 0–8.9%):    median CDI = 28.5 | 18% cooling deserts
    Q4 (high LEP, 14%–64%):  median CDI = 43.1 | 29% cooling deserts

  Kruskal-Wallis: H = 115.2, p < 0.001, η² = 0.051
  High LEP vs Low LEP: U = 202,284, p < 0.001
  Gap: +14.7 median CDI points

Finding: The +14.7 CDI gap between highest and lowest LEP quartiles is
statistically significant but the effect size is modest (η² = 0.051),
meaning LEP explains about 5% of CDI variance. This is consistent with
Phase 2 findings: LEP tracts do not independently form cooling deserts in
the regression model, but once in a cooling desert, the LEP population
faces barriers to emergency communication, cooling center navigation, and
exercising housing rights. LEP compounds risk; it does not initiate it.

8.6 RQ8 — Disability as an Undercounted Risk Factor
----------------------------------------------------
Question: Are high-disability tracts concentrated in cooling deserts,
and does disability independently amplify heat risk within those areas?

Results:
  Disability within cooling deserts:     12.3% (median)
  Disability outside cooling deserts:    10.0% (median)
  Mann-Whitney U = 571,816, p < 0.001

  High-disability tracts: 38% cooling desert rate
  Low-disability tracts:  17% cooling desert rate
  Kruskal-Wallis: H = 293.4, p < 0.001, η² = 0.132

  Logistic regression (Phase 2): pct_disability OR = 0.943, p < 0.001
  → Disability lowers cooling desert odds when income is controlled.

Finding: There is a statistically significant 2.3-percentage-point elevation
in disability rates inside cooling deserts (p < 0.001). The effect size
(η² = 0.132) is moderate — higher than LEP, indicating that disability is a
more concentrated vulnerability. However, the logistic regression shows that
disability does not independently predict where cooling deserts form. The
correct interpretation is: disability is an amplifying condition within cooling
deserts, placing those residents at greatest physiological risk from heat
exposure that higher-income households could manage with available technology.

8.7 RQ10 — Elderly and Children Under Compounded Risk
------------------------------------------------------
Question: Where do age-vulnerable populations face simultaneous heat
exposure and financial constraint?

Results:
  Elderly 65+:
    High elderly tracts (>21.1%):  19% cooling desert rate | CDI = 35.8
    Low elderly tracts:             27% cooling desert rate | CDI = 41.5
    Mann-Whitney p < 0.001
    → Elderly-concentrated tracts have LOWER CDI — older neighborhoods
      are more financially stable. Elderly are physiologically vulnerable
      but not disproportionately in cooling deserts by tract-level analysis.
    Triple compound (high elderly + high burden + HVI ≥ 4): 73 tracts
      Bronx: 15 | Brooklyn: 34 | Queens: 23

  Children under 18:
    High children tracts (>24.6%): 38% cooling desert rate | CDI = 47.6
    Low children tracts:           21% cooling desert rate | CDI = 38.0
    Mann-Whitney p < 0.001
    → Child-concentrated tracts have HIGHER CDI and higher desert rates.
    Triple compound (high children + high burden + HVI ≥ 4): 127 tracts
      Bronx: 66 | Brooklyn: 44 | Queens: 14

Finding: Children and the elderly follow opposite patterns at the
neighborhood level. Elderly-concentrated tracts are modestly better
protected financially — consistent with older, more established residential
areas. Child-concentrated tracts track the poverty and racial burden pattern
strongly: 38% cooling desert rate vs 21% citywide. The 127 tracts combining
high child concentration with high rent burden and high HVI represent a
priority zone for pediatric heat health intervention — overwhelmingly
concentrated in the Bronx (52%) and Brooklyn (35%).

=======================================================================
SECTION 9 — FINDINGS SYNTHESIS
=======================================================================

Nine statistically supported findings, ordered by analytical weight:

1. Race is the dominant predictor of heat vulnerability at the tract level
   (β_std = +0.73, explaining 27.7pp of variance above income, p < 0.001).
   This is not a known-confounder effect — pct_black loads on a separate
   PCA component from income, confirming it captures structurally distinct
   variance.

2. Cooling desert risk is not scattered — it is spatially concentrated
   with exceptional intensity (Moran's I = 0.867, z = 70.81). The entire
   South and Central Bronx constitutes a single contiguous high-risk zone.

3. 557 NYC census tracts (25.0%) meet the cooling desert definition.
   The Bronx holds 54% of them. 66% of all Bronx tracts are spatial
   hot-spots (LISA HH classification).

4. The Cooling Desert Index (CDI) separates deserts from non-deserts
   with AUC = 0.929. The 21.6-point gap between desert and non-desert
   mean CDI scores represents a 1.5 standard deviation effect — strong
   by social science standards.

5. Five structurally distinct risk typologies exist. Clusters 3, 4, and 5
   all qualify as high-risk but for different reasons — immigrant heat
   burden (LEP/overcrowding), racial heat burden (% Black/HVI), and
   compound deprivation (income/disability) — requiring differentiated
   policy responses.

6. 45% of confirmed cooling deserts have no Cool It! site within 0.5 miles.
   The sites that do exist are not preferentially placed in higher-CDI tracts
   (no significant CDI difference between covered and uncovered deserts, p = 1.0).

7. NYCHA tracts face CDI = 53.0 vs private renter tracts at 39.5 (+13.4
   points, p < 0.001) despite lower reported rent burden — identifying
   energy costs as the binding constraint, not subsidized rent.

8. 89% of cooling desert tracts already exceed 86°F average baseline
   temperature, placing their residents in chronic daily danger throughout
   the entire summer, not only during declared heat emergencies.

9. Child-concentrated tracts have a 38% cooling desert rate — nearly
   double the citywide average — and 127 tracts combine high child
   concentration with extreme heat and severe rent burden.

SECTION 10 — LIMITATIONS AND DATA GAPS
=======================================================================

10.1 Known Analytical Limitations
-----------------------------------
  - NYCHVS energy cost variables are not integrated at the tract level.
    Energy burden is a strong theoretical driver of the AC non-use gap
    but cannot be directly modeled. DOE LEAD energy burden data would
    address this limitation.

  - The binary cooling desert definition (HVI ≥ 4 AND burden > 30%)
    uses a fixed threshold. Alternative thresholds (e.g., HVI ≥ 4 AND
    burden > 38.2%, the top quartile) produce a more conservative count
    (211 tracts, 9.5%). Both are analytically defensible; the 557-tract
    definition is used throughout because it is more inclusive and better
    suited for policy planning.

  - Cool It! site analysis uses proximity (0.5-mile radius) as a proxy
    for access. This does not account for actual hours of operation,
    mobility barriers, workforce schedules, or ADA accessibility — all
    known limitations of proximity-based access analysis documented in
    the literature.

  - Emergency indoor cooling center coordinates are not in the dataset.
    The infrastructure access gap analysis covers only outdoor Cool It!
    NYC features, not the full cooling infrastructure network.

  - K-means clustering is sensitive to initialization. Results were
    confirmed across 10 random initializations (n_init = 10) with a fixed
    seed (42) for reproducibility. The cluster structure was stable across
    all initializations.

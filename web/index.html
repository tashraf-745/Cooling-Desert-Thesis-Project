New York City's five boroughs contain 2,231 census tracts — the geographic units used to measure where heat risk, rent burden, and cooling access converge across the city's renter population.

02

Where Heat Strikes Hardest

The NYC Heat Vulnerability Index ranks each neighborhood 1–5 by expected heat mortality risk, combining surface temperature, green space, air conditioning access, and poverty. Rank 4–5 neighborhoods bear a disproportionate share of heat-related deaths.

3× higher heat mortality risk in HVI rank 5 neighborhoods Rosenthal et al., 2014 · DOHMH HVI
03

When You Can't Afford to Cool Down

Rent burden compounds heat risk. Households spending over 50% of income on rent have little left for electricity. The HVI counts AC ownership — but 21% of renters who own air conditioning do not use it due to cost, making high AC ownership a misleading signal of protection.

21% of renters with AC don't use it due to cost NYC Comptroller Energy Insecurity Report, 2022
04

557 Cooling Deserts

When heat vulnerability is high and the household capacity to respond is structurally blocked, a cooling desert emerges. These 557 tracts — 25% of New York City — are places where the system offers no realistic path to cooling.

557 cooling desert tracts — 1.47 million renters Cooling Desert Index · CDI ≥ 0.5, HVI rank ≥ 3
05

Race Is an Independent Risk Factor

After controlling for income and green space, a 1% increase in a tract's Black population is associated with a 0.43°F increase in surface temperature. In Manhattan the effect reaches 5.27°F. Race is not merely a proxy for poverty — it is its own axis of heat exposure.

+0.43°F surface temperature per 1% increase in Black residents Shaker et al., 2019 · Manhattan: +5.27°F
06

The Bronx: Compounded at Scale

Cooling deserts are not isolated pockets. 66% of Bronx tracts are surrounded by other high-risk tracts, forming a continuous geography of compounded disadvantage. The spatial autocorrelation index confirms this pattern is structural, not coincidental.

0.867 Moran's I — cooling deserts cluster systematically PySAL spatial weights · p < 0.001
07

Public Cooling Is Not Enough

NYC operates 218 outdoor Cool It! cooling features — but coverage is uneven. Many cooling desert tracts have no site within walking distance, and 83% of indoor cooling centers are closed on Sundays, when resting heat mortality risk is highest.

83% of cooling centers closed on Sundays NYC Comptroller "Overheated & Underserved," 2022

Methodology

How the Cooling Desert Index was built, what statistical methods were used, and where this analysis has known limits.

01 How the Cooling Desert Index Was Built

The Cooling Desert Index (CDI) is a composite score combining seven census-tract-level variables into a single continuous measure of structural heat vulnerability. Each variable is min-max normalized to 0–100 and multiplied by its assigned weight. The CDI ranges from 2.9 (lowest risk) to 72.0 (highest risk), with a citywide mean of 39.4.

Variables were selected using principal component analysis (PCA). Three components were retained explaining 64.4% of total variance. Variables with redundant axes (poverty and income) or non-significant regression coefficients (Hispanic ethnicity, elderly share) were excluded.

02 Statistical Methods
OLS Regression on HVI Rank
Three nested models tested whether race predicts heat vulnerability independently of income. Model 3 (full specification with borough fixed effects) achieved R² = 0.567 — a +27.7 percentage-point gain over income alone. The standardized β for % Black (0.726) was the strongest predictor in the model. n = 2,197 tracts.
Principal Component Analysis
PCA on 10 candidate variables identified 3 components explaining 64.4% of total variance. Component 1 captures economic deprivation (income, poverty, rent burden). Component 2 captures racial heat exposure. Component 3 captures language and overcrowding barriers. Variables with eigenvalues < 1 or theoretical redundancy were excluded from the CDI.
K-Means Clustering (k = 5)
Tracts were clustered on 7 normalized CDI variables. The optimal k was determined by elbow method on within-cluster sum of squares and silhouette scores. Five typologies emerged with distinct risk profiles: Low Risk, Financially Strained, Immigrant Heat Burden, Racial Heat Burden, and Compound Deprivation.
Spatial Autocorrelation (Moran's I)
Global Moran's I = 0.867 (z = 67.9, p < 0.001, 999 permutations) using PySAL queen contiguity weights. Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) identified 582 HH hot-spots and 516 LL cold-spots at p < 0.05. This is the highest Moran's I of any variable tested in the analysis.

All scripts are reproducible Python (pandas, geopandas, scikit-learn, PySAL, statsmodels). Source code is available in the analysis/ directory of this repository.

04 What This Analysis Cannot Show

Three data gaps limit the scope of this analysis. Future work with these datasets would materially improve the accuracy of the Cooling Desert Index and the policy scenario models.

Energy burden at the tract level
The U.S. Department of Energy's LEAD (Low-Income Energy Affordability Data) tool provides energy burden estimates by census tract. This analysis uses rent burden as a proxy for unaffordable cooling costs, but energy burden data would directly measure whether households can afford to run air conditioning — a more precise measure of the constraint identified in the NYC Comptroller's energy insecurity research.
Emergency indoor cooling center accessibility
This analysis includes outdoor Cool It! NYC features but lacks geocoded data for the city's indoor emergency cooling centers — including their hours of operation, ADA accessibility status, capacity, and actual utilization during heat events. Without this, the "access gap" measure in Panel 7 captures only outdoor cooling proximity, not the full emergency cooling infrastructure.
Building-level heat and hot water failures
NYC's Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) tracks heat and hot water violations at the building level. These violations are concentrated in the same neighborhoods identified as cooling deserts, but the building-level linkage would allow this analysis to connect structural landlord failures directly to individual cooling desert tracts — strengthening the evidence base for enforcement-based interventions.
